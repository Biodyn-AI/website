PROBE An independent evaluation programme from Biodyn

How capable are biological AI models becoming?

Models trained on protein and genome sequence are improving quickly, and the same capability that helps someone design a better enzyme can help someone design a worse pathogen. PROBE measures both, on the same models, with the same yardstick.

Bigger protein models read biological function better, but only up to a point. Past a few hundred million parameters, a hundredfold more size buys nothing we can measure.

One family, ESM2, so the only thing changing along the axis is size. The task is spotting a transcription factor from its amino-acid sequence alone. The red line is what you get with no model at all.

How to read this, and what it does not show

The curve has a knee. Below about 150 million parameters the score rises by roughly 2.8 points for every tenfold increase in size. From 150 million to 15 billion, a hundredfold increase, the total gain is a tenth of a point. The largest model is not the best one: at 15 billion it sits fractionally below the 3 billion model and level with the 650 million, all three inside each other's error bars.

The score is macro-F1 rather than plain accuracy, because it counts the rarer class as heavily as the common one. Bars are 95% intervals over resampled sequence clusters.

This is not our discovery. The Ankh team reported in 2023 that ESM-2 15B did not beat smaller ESM models and gave inconsistent results between runs; a 2025 study in Scientific Reports found every model above 150 million performing comparably; and PFMBench reports the same flatness across far more tasks. Who found what, in full →

There is also a confound in the axis. Every ESM2 model here saw roughly the same number of training tokens regardless of size, so this ladder does not test scale under a budget that grows with the model. A NeurIPS 2024 study that re-allocated that compute beat ESM-2 3B with a larger model on fewer repeated tokens. So this ladder stops paying; protein models in general have not stopped scaling.

And a limit of our own: proteins that resemble each other should not straddle the train and test split, or a model can score well by recognising a near-copy. Our grouping merged less than the field standard of 25 to 30 percent sequence identity, so every protein score here should be read as an upper estimate.

Newer is not automatically better

Held at comparable size, the strongest result on this task belongs to a model from the end of 2020. On this measure the field has been broadly flat for four years while the models themselves changed a great deal.

Five research groups' models, all measured the same way, with size held between 300 and 700 million parameters so the axis is vintage rather than scale.

How firmly to hold that: not very

These intervals overlap almost entirely, so this is an ordering of best estimates rather than a demonstrated ranking. Our own Standard says to report how likely a ranking is to flip rather than publish a bare order, and we have not computed that here yet, so by our own rule the claim is underpowered. Read it as "nothing here has clearly moved" rather than "2020 beat 2023".

An earlier build of this page showed the 2023 model far below the others, which would have read as a sharp decline. That was a bug in how its sequences were spelled for its tokeniser, not a property of the model. It is recorded here because it is exactly the kind of plausible artefact this programme exists to catch.

The question we are built to answer

As models get bigger, does the gap between what a model says and what it internally knows widen, or close? If it closes, judging models by their outputs gets safer as the field advances. If it widens, output-only assessment becomes steadily more misleading exactly as models become more capable.

Why nobody has answered this, and what it needs from us

Two bodies of work exist and do not meet. One measures how models improve as they get bigger and never looks inside them. The other looks inside, usually one model at a time, to settle a particular safety question. The composite needs a ladder of model sizes and an internal read of each one under a fixed budget, which is what this harness does.

Our current numbers hint the gap shrinks with size, but weakly and on a task too easy for the answer to mean much: when scores crowd toward the top, any gap is squeezed shut whether or not anything real is happening. So this is what we are building for, not a result we are reporting.

A benchmark normally scores what a model puts out. PROBE also reads what the model holds internally, using a readout of the same size for both, so a difference is a fact about the model rather than the instrument. Fine-tuning, the strongest version of the measurement, is not yet run on any model here, so what we report today is a lower bound. How the measurement works →

What this page still cannot tell you
  • Ten models from 150 million to 15 billion parameters land between 0.94 and 0.97 on the protein task, so it is running out of room to tell them apart. A harder task is needed before differences at the top mean anything.
  • The size trend covers one model family. The vintage comparison covers five, enough to be suggestive and not enough to be a trend line.
  • The biorisk track has no numbers of ours. It is gated on process, not capability.
  • The gap we report is a difference between two layers. The stronger version, what light fine-tuning recovers, has been measured on a genome model by another group but not by us on any model.
  • The protein split is weaker than the field standard, so those scores are upper estimates.
  • Our shuffled-label test runs 100 shuffles, so the best it can report is 1 in 101, and every result reports exactly that. It shows these scores are not coincidence; it cannot rank them.
  • Intervals come from 60 resamples. Treat their endpoints as coarse.
  • Nothing here has a real task ceiling, so we do not know how much of the remaining distance is even reachable.
  • Genome language models are not covered yet.
  • Coverage is a handful of tasks on one dataset per modality, and the layer result in particular is known to vary by dataset.
