PROBE An independent evaluation programme from Biodyn

How capable are biological AI models becoming?

Models trained on protein and genome sequence are improving quickly, and the same capability that helps someone design a better enzyme can help someone design a worse pathogen. PROBE measures both, on the same models, with the same yardstick, and publishes the trend rather than one-off scores.

This page is the summary. Everything on it is produced by an open harness, and every number carries the baseline it had to beat and the test that could have killed it.

Much of what follows has been found before, by other groups, sometimes on far more evidence than we have, and on one point the published work disagrees with us. We keep an account of that rather than leaving it for a reader to discover.

Bigger protein models read biological function better, but only up to a point. Past a few hundred million parameters, a hundredfold more size buys nothing we can measure.

Each point is one model from the ESM2 family, which shares training data and release date across sizes, so the only thing changing along the axis is scale. The task is telling a transcription factor from a protein that is not one, using only the amino-acid sequence. The red line is what you get from counting amino acids with no model at all. Bars are 95% intervals over resampled sequence clusters.

The score is macro-F1 rather than plain accuracy, because it counts the rarer class as heavily as the common one. On a set where most proteins are not transcription factors, accuracy would flatter a model that simply said "not one" nearly every time.

The curve has a knee, and past it the line is flat. Below about 150 million parameters, the score rises by roughly 2.8 points for every tenfold increase in size. From 150 million to 15 billion, a hundredfold increase, the total gain is a tenth of a point. The largest model in the family is not the best one: at 15 billion parameters it scores fractionally below the 3 billion model and level with the 650 million, and all three sit inside each other's error bars. Even the smallest model, at 8 million parameters, already beats counting amino acids.

None of that is new, and we are not presenting it as new. The Ankh team reported in 2023 that ESM-2 at 15 billion parameters "did not outperform the smaller models from the ESM family in all of the tasks", and that it gave inconsistent results between runs. A 2025 study in Scientific Reports found that on one of its benchmark sets every model of 150 million parameters or more performed comparably, and that ESM-2 150M slightly outperformed ESM-2 3B on mutational-scanning data despite being twenty times smaller. The PFMBench preprint reports similar flatness across a far larger suite of tasks. What this page adds is the same curve measured on a function task with a no-model reference, resampled intervals and a shuffled-label test attached to every point.

There is also a confound in the horizontal axis that we should not hide. Every ESM-2 model here was trained on roughly the same number of tokens regardless of its size, so this ladder does not test scale under a budget that grows with the model. A NeurIPS 2024 study that re-allocated that compute trained a 10.7 billion parameter model on fewer repeated tokens and beat ESM-2 3B on most of its downstream tasks. So the defensible reading is that this particular ladder stops paying, not that protein models have stopped scaling. The ESM-2 authors themselves report continued improvement with size and claim no plateau.

The 15 billion model is too large to run at full precision on the hardware we rented, so it was measured in half precision. That is a difference in the instrument rather than in the model, and it would be an easy way to manufacture a flat curve, so we re-measured the 3 billion model in half precision as a control: its score did not move at all. The flattening is not an artefact of how the biggest model was run.

Separately, for most of these models the most informative layer sits partway through rather than at the end: the final layer was the best one for only one model in twelve. The honest version is narrower than that sounds, though. For six of the twelve, the advantage of the best inner layer survives resampling; for the other six it points the same way but is not distinguishable from noise, and those six include the four highest-scoring models.

On this point the published evidence is genuinely mixed, and one dataset cannot settle it. A 2026 preprint that swept layers across 13 protein language models, 15 tasks and 11 datasets found the deepest layer best in only 17.9% of cases, which agrees with us. The Scientific Reports study cited above swept layers on a different model and found the last layer most commonly best, which does not. Both observe that the answer depends on the dataset, and on whether a task is about a whole protein or about individual residues. Ours is one task on one dataset, so it is a data point in that argument rather than a resolution of it.

Does each generation actually improve on the last?

The chart above varies size within one family. This one varies vintage instead, holding size roughly fixed between 300 and 700 million parameters, so that what moves along the axis is the year a model was published rather than how big it is. Without that restriction a date axis would just be a scaling curve in disguise.

Each point is a different research group's protein language model, all measured on the same task with the same protocol. Labels give the family and size.

Newer is not automatically better

The obvious way to read a field is that each generation improves on the last. Held at comparable size, that is not what these measurements show. The strongest result on this task belongs to a model released at the end of 2020, and every model released since, through to 2023, sits slightly below it. On this measure the field has been broadly flat for about four years while the models themselves have changed a great deal.

How firmly to hold that: not very. These models' intervals overlap almost entirely, so this is an ordering of best estimates rather than a demonstrated ranking. Our own Standard says to report how likely a ranking is to flip rather than publish a bare order, and we have not computed that for this chart yet, so by our own rule this claim is currently underpowered. Read it as "nothing here has clearly moved" rather than as "2020 beat 2023".

It is one task, and none of these models was built specifically for it, so this is not a verdict on any of them. It is a reason to measure rather than assume: a dashboard that plotted release dates and expected a rising line would have drawn the wrong picture, and only a like-for-like comparison at matched size makes that visible at all.

An earlier build of this page showed the 2023 model far below the others, which would have read as a sharp decline. That was a bug in how its sequences were spelled for its tokeniser, not a property of the model, and it is recorded here because it is exactly the kind of plausible artefact this programme exists to catch. The corrected figure is the one shown above.

Two things worth tracking

Capability is not one number. A model can be useful and dangerous through the same understanding, so PROBE follows both and reports them side by side rather than choosing one story.

What these models make possible

Recognising protein function, recovering regulatory structure, reading cell identity. These are the capabilities that make the models worth building, and tracking them shows whether the field is actually progressing or simply getting larger.

in preparation

What they make easier to misuse

The same models learn biology that their outputs never show, and a released checkpoint is a starting point rather than a fixed capability. This track measures how much of that is recoverable, and it is deliberately not live yet: it runs only once the disclosure process and external review described in the Standard are in place.

How the measurement works

A benchmark normally scores what a model puts out. PROBE also reads what the model holds internally, using a readout of exactly the same size for both, so any difference is a fact about the model rather than about the measuring instrument. Where those two answers diverge, the published score is understating the model.

A limit worth stating plainly: fine-tuning is not yet measured on any model here. What we report today is therefore the difference between the best internal layer and the sanctioned output layer, which is a lower bound on what elicitation might recover rather than a measurement of it. It is also, arithmetically, the same quantity as the layer effect described further up this page.

And one about the protein task specifically, which belongs here rather than in a footnote. Proteins that resemble each other should not be split across training and testing, or a model can score well by recognising a near-copy it has already seen. We group sequences before splitting to prevent that, but on this dataset the grouping merged almost nothing — 2302 groups for 2400 proteins — so in practice the split is close to random and the protection is weak. Established protein benchmarks cluster at 25 to 30 percent sequence identity, which is stricter than what we actually achieved. Every protein score on this page should be read as an upper estimate until that is fixed.

The task itself is not one we invented either. A 2025 study did transcription-factor identification with the same family of models and a stricter homology control than ours, clustering at 25% sequence identity over a smaller, balanced set of 1044 proteins. Their own conclusion names ESM2-650M as the best pre-trained model; the 150M-over-650M difference visible in their table sits inside their error bars, and reading it as a scaling result is our inference rather than a claim they make.

What is not yet true

An evaluator that hides its own limits is not one. As of this build:

  • Ten models spanning 150 million to 15 billion parameters all land between 0.94 and 0.97 on this task, close enough together that it is running out of room to tell them apart. The flat scaling curve above should therefore be read as a statement about this task as much as about the models: a harder one is needed before differences at the top mean anything.
  • The size trend covers one model family. The like-for-like comparison across vintages covers five, which is enough to be suggestive and not enough to be a trend line.
  • The biorisk track has no published numbers at all. The measurement is specified and the harness supports it; it is gated on process, not on capability.
  • The protein split is weaker than the field standard. See the note above: our grouping merged almost nothing, so every protein score here is an upper estimate.
  • Our shuffled-label test runs 100 shuffles, so the strongest result it can report is 1 in 101 — and every single result reports exactly that. It is enough to say these scores are not coincidence, and not enough to tell them apart. We are raising the count.
  • The intervals come from 60 resamples, which is few. Treat their endpoints as coarse.
  • Nothing here has a real task ceiling. We compare against the best cheap-feature readout, which most models beat; we do not yet know how much of the remaining gap to a perfect score is even reachable.
  • Genome language models are not covered yet. Neither is any measurement of what light fine-tuning recovers, which is the number the biorisk track needs most, and without it the gap we report is a lower bound rather than a measurement.
  • Coverage is a handful of tasks on one dataset per modality. One dataset cannot show whether any of this generalises, and the layer result in particular is known to vary by dataset.
